The **Load Shedding & Power Outage Management System** is highly relevant in regions that experience power instability (like Bangladesh, South Africa, or parts of South America).

### What is the use of this system?

Currently, power authorities often manage load shedding using manual spreadsheets, and customers find out about outages via chaotic Facebook groups, busy phone lines, or simply when their lights go out.

**The purpose of this system is to:**

1. Automate the complex math of dividing power cuts fairly across different city zones.
2. Provide absolute transparency to the public so they can plan their lives (cooking, studying, running generators) around accurate schedules.
3. Separate *planned* load shedding from *unexpected* hardware faults, routing technicians only to the real emergencies.

---

### Who will use it? (The 5 Key Roles)

1. **The Customer / Resident:** Everyday people and business owners who need to know when their power will go off and want to report unexpected blackouts.
2. **The Power Operator (Control Room):** The engineers who monitor grid demand and generate the load-shedding schedules.
3. **The Technician (Field Worker):** The repair crews dispatched to fix exploded transformers or downed lines.
4. **The Zone Manager:** Supervisors who oversee grid health and technician performance for a specific city district.
5. **The Admin:** IT staff who manage user accounts, add new substations to the database, and maintain the system.

---

### The User Journeys (Step-by-Step Workflows)

Here is how the system works in action for each type of user.

#### Journey 1: The Customer (Planning & Reporting)

* **Step 1:** Customer opens the web/mobile app and enters their exact location or Meter ID.
* **Step 2:** The dashboard displays their current status (e.g., "Status: Normal") and the upcoming week's Load Shedding schedule (e.g., "Planned Outage: Today, 18:00 - 20:00").
* **Step 3:** At 14:00, the customer's power suddenly goes out (this is outside the schedule).
* **Step 4:** The customer taps "Report Outage."
* **Step 5:** The system checks the database. It confirms this is an *unplanned* fault. It logs the ticket and instantly sends the customer an automated SMS: "We have received your report. A technician is being assigned."
* **Step 6:** Once the technician fixes it, the customer receives a final notification: "Power restored."

#### Journey 2: The Power Operator (Algorithmic Scheduling)

* **Step 1:** The Operator logs into the main control room dashboard. The national grid indicates a massive power deficit; they need to shed 50 Megawatts immediately.
* **Step 2:** The Operator clicks "Generate Load Shedding Plan."
* **Step 3:** Your backend algorithm kicks in. It selects groups of Feeders to shut off, ensuring no single area gets cut twice in one day, while completely ignoring "Critical Feeders" (like the ones powering the local Hospital).
* **Step 4:** The Operator reviews the generated schedule and clicks "Publish."
* **Step 5:** The database updates. Redis cache is refreshed. Push notifications/SMS are instantly blasted out to all Customers registered in those specific target zones.

#### Journey 3: The Technician (The Repair Workflow)

* **Step 1:** The Technician is sitting in their truck. Their mobile app pings: "New Fault Assigned: Substation 4, Transformer blown."
* **Step 2:** The Technician opens the app, sees the GPS location, and taps "Accept & En Route." (This changes the outage status on the Customer's dashboard to "Technician Dispatched").
* **Step 3:** The Technician arrives at the site and taps "Repairing."
* **Step 4:** After 2 hours of work, they fix the transformer. They take a photo of the repair, upload it to the app, and tap "Resolved."
* **Step 5:** The system automatically updates the Zone Manager's dashboard and notifies the affected Customers that power is back.

Journey 4: The Zone Manager (Analytics & Oversight)
Step 1: The Manager logs in and views the Live Grid Status Dashboard.

Step 2: Instead of a map, they see a real-time, sortable Shadcn UI data table listing all Zones and Feeders. Each row features a distinct status badge: a red "Unexpected Fault" badge, a yellow "Planned Outage" badge, or a green "Online" badge.

Step 3: The Manager filters the table to view only "Unexpected Faults" to ensure technicians are actively assigned to those specific Feeders.

Step 4: At the end of the month, they pull an analytics report to answer key questions: "Which Feeder line fails the most often?" or "What is the average time it takes for Technician Team A to resolve a ticket?"

Step 5: Based on the data from these tables, the Manager formally requests infrastructure upgrades for the worst-performing Feeders.

#### Journey 5: The System Itself (The "Smart" Conflict Resolution)

*This happens entirely in the backend and is the coolest part of the project.*

* **Step 1:** An area is currently undergoing *Planned* Load Shedding from 12:00 to 14:00.
* **Step 2:** At 12:15, an uninformed Customer logs in and clicks "Report Power Outage."
* **Step 3:** Instead of creating a repair ticket and bothering a Technician, your backend intercepts the request.
* **Step 4:** It queries the database, realizes the Customer's exact location is currently under a scheduled blackout, and instantly auto-replies to the user: *"Your area is currently under scheduled load shedding until 14:00. This is not a fault. No technician will be dispatched."*




Frontend & User Interface (Web App)

Next.js (App Router): Powers the web interfaces and role-guarded portals (Customer, Operator, Manager, Admin) using server components.

Tailwind CSS & Shadcn UI: Provides rapid styling and pre-built, accessible components (data tables, status badges, modal forms) to create clean, data-heavy dashboards for the Operator and Zone Manager without needing a map.

Backend & Algorithmic Engine

Node.js & Express.js: Hosts the core REST API, executing the fair-rotation load-shedding algorithms and the automated customer report conflict-resolution engine.

Socket.io: Powers bidirectional WebSocket communication to broadcast instantaneous status updates to the Zone Manager's dashboard tables when a technician accepts or resolves an outage.

Node-cron: A lightweight task scheduler running inside Express to trigger automated background jobs, such as simulating national grid power fluctuations or running the hourly load-shedding script.

Database & Caching Layer

Neon Serverless PostgreSQL: Handles relational grid hierarchies using simple Foreign Keys (Zone_ID → Feeder_ID → Meter_ID) instead of complex spatial data.

Prisma ORM: Provides type-safe database queries, automated schema migrations, and simplified relational joins for your Express backend.

Upstash Redis: Delivers fast in-memory caching for published load-shedding schedules, protecting the Postgres database from read-heavy traffic spikes during planned blackouts.

Auth & Notifications

Auth.js (NextAuth): Enforces strict Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) via session tokens, ensuring customers cannot access the Operator control room or Technician ticket queues.

Twilio API: Delivers automated SMS text alerts to impacted customers when their specific Feeder_ID is scheduled for an outage or restored by a technician.
